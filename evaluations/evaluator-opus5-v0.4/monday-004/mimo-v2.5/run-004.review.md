## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**The business is headline-stable but underneath the surface there are three urgent issues:**

1. **SMB starter churn is severe.** Four of nine SMB starter customers (44%) churned in October, representing €10,300/mo in lost MRR. Only €800/mo of net MRR was added from five new starters — meaning the plan is running flat on MRR while burning support and infrastructure resources on high-churn relationships.

2. **Soylent is a churn risk hiding inside the enterprise plan.** Usage dropped 27%, support tickets tripled (3 → 8, including a critical incident), and the invoice amount decreased from €22,000 to €19,500. At €234,000 ARR, this is the highest-risk customer in the book.

3. **Net MRR from the existing book is shrinking.** Starting MRR of €746,100 in September is now €732,400 in current post-churn terms — a gross retention rate of 96.3%. The business is relying on new logos to offset erosion, not to grow.

**The three decisions:**
- Reducing investment in SMB (specifically starter) is well-supported by the data.
- Increasing spend on any single acquisition channel is **not supported** — we lack spend data and full-funnel conversion data.
- The Pro plan deserves the most engineering attention — highest margin, growing, and customers are already bumping plan limits.

## 5. RECOMMENDATIONS

### Decision 1: Reduce investment in one customer segment

**Recommendation: Yes — reduce investment in SMB starter customers, but not SMB as a whole.**

- **SMB starter** (9 current customers, €21,700 MRR) is economically unsustainable: 44% monthly churn rate, €792/mo support cost per customer, only €792/mo profit per customer.
- **SMB pro** (5 current customers, €18,200 MRR) is healthy: zero churn, growing usage, potential upgrade path to enterprise.
- All new customer acquisition in both months came through SMB. **Abandoning SMB entirely would cut off the top of the funnel.** The recommendation is to shift resources away from acquiring new starter-tier customers and toward (a) migrating existing starter customers to pro, (b) improving the starter-to-pro upgrade path, or (c) accepting that some churn is inevitable and reducing the support investment per starter customer.

**Immediate action:** Flag the 5 remaining active SMB starter customers for proactive outreach (bluth, dunder, sterling, monsters, clampett). Any with usage decline >15% should trigger an intervention.

### Decision 2: Increase spend on one acquisition channel

**Recommendation: Do not increase spend on any channel yet. The data does not support this decision.**

- **No spend data is provided**, making CAC calculation impossible.
- **Trial-to-paid conversion is not tracked**, so we can't measure the full-funnel economics of any channel.
- **Paid search** is the most attractive on volume, but lead-to-trial conversion declined 24% as volume scaled. The October attributed revenue is inflated by €174K in expansion bookings from existing customers, not new acquisition.
- **Content** produced the most new logos (3 in 2 months) with stable conversion rates and lower volume. This may be more capital-efficient, but we can't prove it without spend data.
- **Partner** has the best conversion rate (~24%) but the smallest scale.

**What leadership should do instead:**
1. Request channel spend data for September and October from the marketing team.
2. Implement trial-to-paid tracking in the funnel before making any spend decisions.
3. Only then calculate CAC, CAC payback period, and channel ROI.
4. In the interim, hold spend flat and monitor paid search lead quality over 1–2 more months.

### Decision 3: Which plan deserves the most engineering attention

**Recommendation: The Pro plan.**

Engineering should prioritize:
1. **Usage visibility and overage management** — Wonka and Aperture are well above included limits with no apparent billing mechanism. Build usage dashboards, threshold alerts, and automated overage invoicing.
2. **Self-serve upgrade path** — Create a frictionless pro → enterprise upgrade flow. This directly addresses the usage overages and moves customers into a higher-LTV plan.
3. **Rate-limit tooling** — Rate-limit tickets are the #1 support category for pro customers. Better client-side rate-limit handling, retry logic, and usage forecasting would reduce ticket volume.

**Secondary priorities:**
- Enterprise: Investigate why infrastructure costs are growing with flat/declining request volume. The Acme batch migration should have reduced interactive infra load; if it didn't, there's a cost model problem.
- Starter: Invest in churn prediction (usage decline monitoring) and an automated pro-plan upgrade nudge when usage consistently exceeds 80% of included requests.

### Additional Immediate Actions

1. **Wayne Enterprises renewal (Nov 30):** Assign an executive sponsor immediately. Two open/pending high-severity tickets (performance, integration) are a red flag. Schedule a QBR before the renewal date.
2. **Soylent risk mitigation:** The –27% usage decline + 8 support tickets (including critical) is the strongest churn signal in the data. Assign dedicated support and a customer success review this week.
3. **Soylent invoice discrepancy:** Investigate why September's invoice was €22,000 vs. the expected €19,500. This affects revenue accuracy and may indicate a billing configuration issue.

## 6. UNCERTAINTIES / DATA ISSUES

### Confirmed Data Quality Problems

1. **Soylent duplicate invoice.** Transaction INV-2026-10-0209 appears twice in revenue.csv: once from Stripe (Oct 1, €19,500) and once from billing_sync (Oct 2, €19,500). These are the same underlying invoice. If any revenue report sums raw invoices, October revenue is **overstated by €19,500**. The plans data appears to de-duplicate correctly (enterprise plan MRR of €659,500 includes soylent at €19,500 once).

2. **Soylent September overcharge.** September invoice was €22,000, but current ARR (€234,000) implies €19,500/mo. The €2,500 gap is unexplained. Possible causes: a one-time overage charge (unlikely — usage was 980K, well within the 3M enterprise limit), a mid-month rate change, or a billing error. **This should be reconciled.**

3. **Gringotts annual invoice distorts monthly comparisons.** The €120,000 annual subscription invoice in October creates a cash-revenue spike that does not reflect monthly performance. Any revenue dashboard comparing Sep to Oct totals will show misleading growth unless this is normalized. The plans data already converts to monthly equivalent (€10,000/mo), but raw invoice reports likely do not.

### Unsupported Causal Claims to Watch For

4. **"Paid search is our best channel"** — The attributed revenue is dominated by expansion bookings from existing customers (€174K out of €238.8K), not new customer acquisition. Attributed revenue ≠ acquisition value.

5. **"Acme's migration freed up capacity"** — Acme's requests dropped 45%, but enterprise plan infrastructure costs rose 3.1%. Either the migration didn't reduce interactive load as expected, or infrastructure costs are not closely tied to request volume. Without a breakdown of batch vs. interactive infra costs, this claim is unverifiable.

6. **"Content converts better than paid search"** — Content has a higher lead-to-trial rate, but we don't know trial-to-paid rates, time-to-close, or the actual contract values. "Better" cannot be established without full-funnel data and spend data.

### Metrics That Cannot Be Calculated

7. **CAC or CAC payback by channel** — No channel spend data. The CFO's specific request cannot be fulfilled. Required: ad spend, sales team allocation by channel, and full conversion funnel data.

8. **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)** — While we can approximate a monthly MRR-based retention figure, true NRR requires cohort tracking over 12 months. Two months of data is insufficient.

9. **LTV by segment** — With only 2 months of data and no historical churn rates by segment (we only see October churns), customer lifetime cannot be reliably estimated.

10. **Support cost per plan accuracy** — The plans data shows support costs that include "fully loaded cost of customer-success staff assigned to it," but we don't know how staff are allocated. The sharp increase in enterprise support costs (€159K → €168K) with no new customers suggests either new hires, reallocation, or a timing difference in cost recognition.
